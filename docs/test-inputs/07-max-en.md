How to Make Learning Stick: What the Science of Memory Tells Us

1. Introduction

Many people study hard and still forget most of what they learned soon after the exam is over. This is not a sign of weak willpower. It is simply how human memory works. The good news is that psychologists have spent more than a century studying what helps memories last, and their findings point to a handful of practical habits. This guide collects the methods that busy working adults can use even when they only have a few minutes at a time. For each method, we look at what it is, why it works, and how to try it in daily life.

2. Forgetting Is Normal

In 1885, the German psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus published a book about his experiments on memory and forgetting. He memorized lists of meaningless syllables and then measured how much less effort it took to learn the same list again after some time had passed. His results showed that memory fades fastest right after learning, and that the rate of forgetting slows down as time goes on. A graph of this pattern is known as the forgetting curve.

The forgetting curve teaches two lessons. First, much of what we learn will disappear within days unless we do something about it. Second, if we bring the material back to mind while we are in the middle of forgetting it, we forget it more slowly the next time. The goal of studying, then, is not to memorize something perfectly in one sitting. The goal is to recall it again and again, each time just as it starts to slip away.

3. The Trap of Rereading

The most common way to prepare for a test is to read the textbook and notes again from the beginning. When we reread, the sentences feel familiar and flow easily, so we feel that we know the material. But a feeling of familiarity is not the same as being able to recall something on our own. Mistaking ease of reading for real understanding is called the fluency illusion.

Highlighting has a similar problem. While we are highlighting, it feels like hard work, but if we color large parts of the page without deciding what really matters, little of it stays in memory. This does not mean rereading and highlighting are useless. The danger is believing that they are enough.

4. Retrieval Practice: Pulling Information Out

Retrieval means bringing stored information back to mind by yourself. Closing the book and trying to recall what you just read, answering practice questions, and writing down everything you know on a blank sheet of paper are all forms of retrieval practice. We usually think of tests only as a way to measure learning. However, researchers have shown many times that the act of taking a test makes memories stronger. This is known as the testing effect.

In a study published in 2006, the American psychologists Roediger and Karpicke asked students to study short prose passages. One group read the passages several times. Another group read them once and then practiced writing down what they could remember. Right after studying, the rereading group scored slightly higher. One week later, however, the group that had practiced recalling remembered more. Rereading looked better in the short term, but retrieval practice produced learning that lasted.

One explanation is that the effort of searching memory strengthens the path to that memory. For this reason, a question that takes some effort to answer is more useful than one that comes to mind instantly. Getting an answer wrong is fine as long as you check the correct answer right away. Flashcards are the simplest tool for retrieval practice. Look at the question on the front, try to recall the answer, and only then turn the card over. Those few seconds of trying to remember before you look are when the learning actually happens.

5. Spaced Repetition: A Little, Many Times

If you spend the same total amount of time studying, spreading it across several days leads to better long-term memory than cramming it into one session. This is called the spacing effect, and Ebbinghaus observed it in his own experiments. Staying up all night before an exam may get you through the next morning, but a month later most of that material will be gone.

Spaced repetition puts this principle to work by gradually increasing the gaps between reviews. For example, you might review something you learned today again tomorrow, then a few days later, and then after an even longer gap. Material you know well gets longer gaps, while material you struggle with gets shorter ones. Many flashcard apps use this approach to decide which cards to show and when. The key is to review just as you are starting to forget. If you review too often, recall is too easy and the benefit shrinks. If you wait too long, you have forgotten almost everything and must learn it from scratch.

6. Interleaving: Mixing It Up

Textbook exercises usually group problems by chapter, so you solve many problems of the same type in a row. Because you can copy the method from the previous problem, this kind of practice feels smooth and easy. It is called blocked practice. Interleaving means mixing different types of problems together instead.

With interleaving, you must first decide which method each problem needs before you can solve it. As a result, practice feels harder and you make more mistakes along the way. Yet several studies have found that people who practiced with mixed problems did better later on problems they had never seen before. Real tests and real work rarely arrive neatly sorted by chapter. If you are learning a foreign language, for example, practicing sentences that mix several grammar points is a form of interleaving, while drilling a single grammar point all day is blocked practice.

7. Elaboration: Asking Why

Elaboration means connecting new information to what you already know and explaining it in more detail. The easiest way to do this is to ask yourself why something is true or how it happens, and then answer in your own words. Suppose you read that rising interest rates tend to reduce borrowing. Ask yourself why. If you can explain that borrowing becomes more expensive when interest goes up, so fewer people want to take out loans, the idea will last longer than a sentence you simply memorized.

Elaboration works especially well when you link new ideas to your own experiences. Connecting a concept to something that happened at work, a story you saw in the news, or another subject you studied gives your memory more hooks to hold on to. Be careful, though. If you invent plausible answers without checking them, you may end up remembering something false very firmly. Always confirm your explanations against the original text.

8. Combining Words and Pictures

The Canadian psychologist Allan Paivio proposed that memory has separate channels for verbal information and visual information. This idea is known as dual coding theory. If you store the same idea both as words and as an image, you have two routes to find it later, so you can still reach the memory even if one route fails.

To use this principle, turn a written explanation into a simple drawing or table after you read it. Draw a process as a flow chart with arrows, show a comparison as a table, and lay out a sequence of events as a timeline. You do not need to draw well. The act of translating words into a picture forces you to think again about how the ideas relate to each other, and that thinking is the learning. The reverse also works. When you see a chart or diagram, try explaining it out loud in words.

9. Finding Concrete Examples

Abstract ideas can feel clear while you read them and then quickly fade. Finding at least one concrete example for each concept makes it easier both to remember and to apply. If you learn the economic term opportunity cost, for instance, create an example from your own life. Spending two hours at the gym after work means giving up the studying you could have done in that time. Examples you build yourself tend to stick better than the ones printed in the textbook.

10. Knowing What You Know

Strong learners have a fairly accurate sense of what they know and what they do not. This ability to step back and examine your own thinking is called metacognition. Without it, people keep reviewing material they already know well while skipping the parts they actually need to work on.

The best way to build metacognition is retrieval practice. Material that feels familiar before you test yourself often turns out to be hard to recall when you actually try. That moment of getting stuck is when you discover a gap in your knowledge. When you study with flashcards, be honest about whether you know each card, and gather the cards you missed to review them again. If you only half remember a card, it is safer to mark it as unknown. Marking it as known means you lose the chance to see it again.

11. Sleep and Rest Are Part of Learning

Sleep is widely believed to play an important role in organizing what we learned during the day and turning it into lasting memory. Cutting back on sleep to study longer may therefore help less than you expect. Material memorized during an all night session is also hard to recall the next day with a tired mind.

12. What the Research Recommends

In 2013, the American psychologist John Dunlosky and his colleagues reviewed a large body of research on common study techniques. They rated practice testing, which is retrieval practice, and distributed practice, which is spaced repetition, as the most useful methods. Rereading, highlighting, and summarizing were judged to be less useful, or useful only in certain situations.

Based on this work, a simple study routine looks like this. First, read the new material once to get the big picture. Next, close the book and try to recall the main points or answer questions about them. Check the parts where you got stuck, and explain to yourself why they are true. From the next day on, recall the material again at increasing intervals, and mix related topics together when you practice.

13. Using Spare Moments

For working adults, setting aside long blocks of study time is hard. Fortunately, the methods described above actually suit short sessions well. Retrieval practice needs only the few seconds it takes to answer one question, and spaced repetition works better when reviews are split into many short sessions across the day.

On the train to work, you might flip through the cards you studied yesterday and try to recall each answer. During a short break after lunch, you could review only the cards you marked as unknown that morning. On the way home, you might make new cards from an article you read today and go through them once. In this way, you recall the same material three separate times in one day, which is more likely to stick than a single hour of study.

14. Conclusion

What the lasting study methods have in common is that they feel harder than the easy ones. Recalling feels harder than rereading, spacing feels harder than cramming, and mixed practice feels harder than repeating the same kind of problem. Researchers call these desirable difficulties, because they make learning feel slower in the moment while making memory stronger in the end.

There is no need to treat forgetting as failure. Each time you pull back something you were starting to forget, the memory becomes a little more durable. Try it now. Without looking back at this guide, recall one idea you just read. That small act is the easiest way to begin learning that lasts.
